## The 7 a.m. surprise

It's the first properly cool morning of the season. You grab a jacket you haven't worn since last winter, start the car, and there it is: the amber horseshoe with an exclamation point. The tire pressure light. By lunch it's gone. What happened?

South Florida's wet season ends in mid-October; the National Weather Service in Miami defines it as May 15 to October 15. After that come the dry months and the cold fronts that sweep down the peninsula. NWS notes that inland areas are the most likely to see freezing temperatures, mainly in the middle of January. NWS also notes that overnight temperatures rarely drop below freezing in South Florida, especially along the coasts. But a front doesn't need to bring a freeze to matter. In the 2024–25 winter, January was the coolest in 15 years, and Fort Lauderdale/Hollywood's lowest reading was 46°F, NWS Miami's winter summary says. After a summer of 90-degree afternoons, that's the coolest air your tires have felt in months.

## What the cold does to the air in your tires

The air in a tire is a fixed amount of gas. Cool it and its pressure drops; warm it and the pressure rises. NHTSA's winter guidance says it simply: as the outside temperature drops, so does tire inflation pressure.

USTMA gives the size of the effect: inflation pressure goes up in warm weather and down in cold weather by **one to two psi for every 10 degrees** of temperature change. Try it with your own numbers:

## The math: why the front usually isn't the whole story

Here's where it gets interesting. The federal standard for tire pressure monitoring, FMVSS No. 138, requires the warning when a tire is **25 percent or more below** the vehicle maker's recommended cold pressure. Automakers can warn earlier, but that's the required line.

Take a car with a **35 psi** door placard. Twenty-five percent below that is about **26 psi**. Now an example, using USTMA's rule of thumb (the temperatures are illustrative, not a forecast):

| Scenario | Starting pressure | Overnight drop of 30°F | Morning pressure | Light? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Set correctly to the placard | 35 psi | 3 to 6 psi | 29 to 32 psi | Probably not |
| Already 4 psi low | 31 psi | 3 to 6 psi | 25 to 28 psi | Quite possibly |
| Already 6 psi low | 29 psi | 3 to 6 psi | 23 to 26 psi | Very likely |

A correctly filled tire usually stays above the line even after a big temperature drop. The tires that trip the light are the ones that were **already low**. Maybe they were set on a hot afternoon, or they've been slowly losing air for weeks, which all tires do. The front didn't cause the problem. It revealed it.

NHTSA's own tire page says much the same: on cold mornings the TPMS light may come on briefly and then turn off, likely because of marginally low pressure that dips below the warning threshold overnight and rises again as the tires warm up.

That's the useful part: the cold-morning light is an early, free inspection. Take it seriously.

## Why it goes off by lunchtime

As you drive, the tires flex and warm up, and the sun heats the pavement and the car. The pressure climbs back a few psi and crosses back over the warning line, so the light goes out.

The tire is no fuller than it was at 7 a.m. Tonight it will cool, and tomorrow morning the light will probably be back. Our [TPMS light guide](/learn/pressure/tpms-light) explains this and what a flashing light means.

## What to do that morning

1. **Check all four with a gauge while they're cold.** That's the morning, before you've driven more than a short distance. USTMA defines cold as parked for at least three hours.
2. **Fill to the door placard number,** not the maximum on the sidewall. The placard is on the driver's door pillar.
3. **Check the spare too.** It loses pressure just like the others.
4. **Note which tire was lowest.** If the same one is low again in a week or two, look for a nail, a damaged valve or a leaking bead. A slow leak won't fix itself when it warms up.
5. **Don't let air out on a warm afternoon.** The placard pressure is a cold pressure. A higher reading after driving is normal.

If you fill at a gas station after a drive, the tires are already warm. Add a little above what you'd normally set, then recheck cold the next morning. Our guide to [tire pressure and temperature](/learn/pressure/tire-pressure-temperature) walks through it.

## When it isn't the weather

Some signs point to something other than a cool morning:

- **The light flashes for a minute or so at startup, then stays on.** That's a system fault, often a sensor battery or a damaged sensor, not low pressure.
- **One tire is far lower than the others,** or drops again within days.
- **The light stays on after you've filled all four** to the placard and driven a few miles.

## Why a gauge beats waiting for the light

The cold-front light is a reminder that TPMS is built to catch a big pressure loss, not to keep your pressure right. A tire can run well below the placard all summer with the dash dark.
