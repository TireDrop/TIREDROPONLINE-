## "It's barely two years old"

It's one of the most common surprises at the tire counter. The car is new, the payments have barely started, and the original tires are already near the wear bars. Owners reasonably ask whether something is wrong with the tires, the car or the way they drive.

Usually it's a mix, and most of it is explainable. Here's our view of why first sets sometimes wear early, based on what tire makers and regulators publish, and what to do about it.

## A factory tire is chosen for the whole car

When a vehicle maker picks tires for a new model, it's balancing a long list: ride comfort, steering feel, wet and dry grip, cabin noise, fuel economy or range, and tread life. No tire maximizes all of them at once. If a carmaker leans toward a quiet ride, crisp handling or efficiency, tread life may not be the top priority.

Michelin is direct about one case: for electric vehicles, carmakers often **prioritize range when optimizing original-equipment tires**, and that comes with trade-offs such as less grip or greater tire wear.

That isn't a flaw. It's a choice made for the car as delivered. The good news is that at replacement time, you get to make your own choice.

## What actually wears a tire

Goodyear's tread life warranty terms list the things that affect how long tread lasts: **tire type, tread compound, construction, the vehicle, maintenance, road and weather conditions, and driving habits.** Some of those you chose when you bought the car. Several you control every day.

**Weight and torque.** Michelin says vehicle weight is a critical factor in how fast a tire wears. EVs add battery weight and instant torque, and Michelin puts their average wear at about **20% faster** than comparable gas vehicles. Our post on [EV tire wear](/blog/ev-tire-wear-budget) covers how to budget for it.

**Heat and pressure.** NHTSA explains that underinflation, excessive speed and overloading cause heat build-up in a tire. South Florida's heat adds to it. A tire run a few psi low wears faster and unevenly.

**Missed rotations.** Front and rear tires do different jobs and wear at different rates. Without rotation, one pair wears out well before the other. Tesla's Model Y manual, for example, asks for rotation every **6,250 miles**, or sooner if tread depths differ by 2/32 inch or more. Where a manual gives no interval, USTMA's recommendation is every **5,000 to 8,000 miles**.

**Alignment.** AAA notes that hitting a pothole can knock the wheels out of alignment, and that uneven tire wear can point to worn or damaged parts. A misaligned car can scrub one edge of a tire for thousands of miles before anyone notices.

**Driving style.** Hard launches, fast cornering and heavy braking all take rubber off. That's especially true with instant torque.

## Read the wear, not just the depth

How the tires wore tells you more than how fast. Look across the tread of each tire:

| What you see | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Both edges worn more than the center | Running under-inflated | Check pressure monthly, cold, at the placard number |
| Center worn more than the edges | Running over-inflated | Use the placard, not the sidewall maximum |
| One edge worn, or feathered ribs | Alignment | Alignment check before the new set |
| Front pair much more worn than rear | No rotation | Rotate on the manual's schedule |
| Even wear, just fast | Tire choice, weight, torque or driving style | Choose the next set with tread life in mind |

## Could it be a defect?

Fast, even wear is usually down to the tire's design and how the car is used. A defect is different, and it tends to show up as something you can see or feel rather than just shallow tread. Signs worth a closer look:

- **A bulge, bubble or separation** anywhere on the tire.
- **A vibration** that started suddenly and doesn't go away after balancing.
- **One tire wearing very differently** from its partner on the same axle, with pressure and alignment checked.

What to do:

1. **Check for recalls.** NHTSA's recall lookup covers both vehicles and tires. Have the tire's DOT code handy.
2. **Have the tire inspected** and keep the findings in writing.
3. **Read the tire's warranty terms.** Tread life coverage and workmanship coverage are usually separate, and each has its own conditions.

## Choosing the second set

This is where you get to set the priorities.

1. **Match the specification.** Same size, and a load index and speed rating at or above what the placard calls for. EVs and some heavier vehicles may need an XL or HL load rating.
2. **Decide what matters most.** If the first set wore too fast for you, look for a tire whose maker puts more weight on tread life, and check its mileage warranty, if it has one.
3. **Use the UTQG treadwear grade carefully.** NHTSA's consumer guide explains that the grade compares a tire's wear with a control tire on a test course, and that real-world results can differ significantly. Compare grades within one brand, not as miles.
4. **Don't give up wet grip.** In South Florida rain, a long-wearing tire still needs strong wet braking.

## Make the next set last

- **Check pressure monthly,** cold, against the door placard.
- **Rotate on schedule,** and keep the records; tread life warranties such as Goodyear's ask for them.
- **Get an alignment** after a hard hit or when you see one-sided wear. We offer [wheel alignment](/services/wheel-alignment) at our Sunrise shop.
- **Measure tread** every few months, and plan to replace at 4/32 for rainy-season grip.
