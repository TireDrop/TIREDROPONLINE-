## Same size, very different price

Search your size and the list can run from modest to eye-opening, all for tires that fit the same wheel. It's natural to wonder whether the expensive one is better or just more expensive.

The short version: a tire size tells you the tire's shape and nothing else. Two tires in the same size can be built for different jobs, carry different loads, be rated for different speeds and use different rubber. Here's what actually separates them, so you can decide which differences are worth paying for on your car.

## First, the parts that aren't optional

Before price enters the picture, three things are set by your vehicle maker and printed on the door placard: **size, load index and speed rating.** USTMA's guidance is that replacement tires match the size, load index and speed rating your vehicle maker recommends. Michelin adds that a tire's load rating must always meet or exceed the vehicle maker's value.

So a lower-priced tire with a lower load index than your placard isn't a bargain. It isn't a match. Check any candidate in our [load and speed rating check](/load-speed-check) before you compare prices at all.

## 1. Tire type

The biggest split is what the tire is built to do. Bridgestone describes all-season tires as designed for dry and wet roads and some snow, a practical choice where conditions aren't extreme, while summer performance tires are built for more grip and sharper handling in warm weather. All-terrain, highway, touring and winter tires each make their own trade-offs.

Two tires in the same size, one touring and one high-performance summer, aren't competing for the same job. Compare within the type that suits how and where you drive.

## 2. Load capacity: standard load versus extra load

Many sizes come in a **standard load (SL)** version and an **extra load (XL)** version. Michelin explains that XL tires have a reinforced internal structure that lets them carry more weight, at higher inflation pressures, than a standard-load tire of the same size. More structure means more material and engineering, and usually a different price.

If your placard calls for XL, you need XL. If it calls for standard load, an XL tire is an option, but its pressure has to come from the right load and inflation information, not guesswork. Ask a tire professional before switching.

## 3. Speed rating

The letter after the load index is the speed rating. Michelin defines it as the maximum speed at which a tire can carry its maximum load under specified conditions: for example, **H** is 130 mph, **V** is 149 mph, **W** is 168 mph and **Y** is 186 mph.

Higher speed ratings usually come with construction aimed at handling. Michelin notes that high-performance tires with higher speed ratings are optimized for better control and a stiffer, more precise ride. That's a real difference, but it's one you're choosing, not one your car needs beyond the placard rating.

## 4. Special construction: run-flat and more

Some tires add structure for a specific purpose:

- **Run-flat tires.** Michelin's run-flats have reinforced sidewalls designed to carry the vehicle's weight after a loss of pressure, for a limited distance at reduced speed, and Michelin's guidance is to fit them only to vehicles approved for them and equipped with a tire pressure monitoring system.
- **Vehicle-specific versions.** Some tires are built to a vehicle maker's specification and carry a marking for it. Tesla, for example, uses T-marked tires; our guide to [T-marked and acoustic tires](/learn/tesla/tesla-t-marked-acoustic-tires) explains what that means.

Each of these is a tire doing more than a basic tire of the same size, and it's priced accordingly.

## 5. Tread compound and design

What you can't see from the size is the rubber itself and the pattern cut into it. Bridgestone points to features like sipes, slots and high-silica compounds as contributors to balanced performance in changing weather. Compound and tread design are where a tire maker trades off wet grip, wear, noise and rolling resistance, and they're a large part of why two same-size tires behave differently on a wet road.

## 6. UTQG grades: useful, with limits

Most passenger tires carry three Uniform Tire Quality Grading marks: **treadwear, traction and temperature.** Try reading a set here:

NHTSA's consumer guide explains what they are, and what they aren't:

- **Treadwear** is a comparative rating from a controlled test course, not a mileage promise.
- **Traction** runs AA, A, B, C, from highest to lowest.
- **Temperature** runs A, B, C, for resistance to heat buildup in a lab test.
- The grades are **assigned by the manufacturers themselves** under a self-certification system.

That last point is why we suggest comparing treadwear inside one brand rather than across brands. Our guide to [UTQG ratings](/learn/sidewall/utqg-ratings) goes deeper.

## 7. The warranty behind it

Many tire lines carry a **limited treadwear (mileage) warranty**. Michelin, for example, says its passenger and light truck replacement tires are covered by a limited mileage warranty that varies by tire line. The miles, the conditions and what you have to do to claim (rotation records, for instance) differ from line to line, so read the actual warranty for the tire you're considering.

A longer warranty isn't a promise of a longer life on your car. It does tell you what the maker is willing to stand behind in writing.

## How to compare prices without fooling yourself

1. **Filter for your placard's size, load index and speed rating** first.
2. **Pick the tire type** that suits your roads and climate.
3. **Line up two to four candidates** on the [compare page](/compare), and read the specs side by side.
4. **Weigh what matters for your driving.** Heavy rain where you live? Look hard at the traction grade and the tire type. High annual miles? Look at the treadwear grade and the warranty terms.
5. **Compare installed cost,** not tire price. Mounting, balancing, valve stems, TPMS service and disposal can all be separate lines. Our comparison of [buying online versus at a local shop](/blog/buy-tires-online-vs-local-shop) lists what to ask for.
6. **Read the date codes** when the tires arrive.

## The bottom line

Same-size tires cost different amounts because they aren't the same tire. Once the non-negotiables are met, the price differences buy you choices: a different type, more load capacity, a higher speed rating, special construction, different rubber, or a different warranty. Decide which of those your driving actually uses, and pay for those.
