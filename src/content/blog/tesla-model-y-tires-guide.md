## Why this guide doesn't have a top five

Search "best tires for Tesla Model Y" and you'll get ranked lists. This isn't one. We don't rank brands, and a list that ignores your wheel size, your model year and how you drive isn't much use anyway.

What we can do is hand you the checklist Tesla and the tire makers actually publish, so you can judge any tire for yourself. Once you know what to look for, the right tire usually narrows down to a short list on its own.

## Step 1: Get your exact size, not "the Model Y size"

There isn't one Model Y tire size. Tesla's manual lists different sizes for different wheel packages, and some packages are **staggered**: the rear tires are wider than the fronts.

So start at the car:

- **Door placard.** The Tire and Loading Information label on the driver's door pillar lists the original size, load and pressure.
- **Owner's manual.** The Wheels and Tires section lists every approved size for your car, with a minimum load index and speed rating for each.
- **The tires themselves.** Useful for confirming, but if a previous owner changed wheels, they may not match the placard.

Tesla's manual is direct about this: use only tires and wheels that match the original specification, and never exceed the speed rating shown on your tires' sidewalls. USTMA says the same for any vehicle: match the placard's size, load-carrying capacity and speed rating, with a load index at or above what the placard requires.

## Step 2: Understand the load rating on an EV

This is where the Model Y differs from the compact crossover it's often compared with.

Michelin explains that EVs put three extra demands on tires:

1. **Weight.** Battery packs add weight, which can require **XL** (extra load) or **HL** (high load) ratings that an equivalent gas vehicle wouldn't need. Per Michelin, HL tires carry more load than XL at the same inflation pressure.
2. **Instant torque.** Electric motors deliver torque immediately, which puts extra stress on the tread during acceleration.
3. **Noise.** Without an engine, road noise from the tires stands out in the cabin.

For your purposes, the rule is simple. Find the load index and any XL or HL marking on the tires Tesla specifies for your size, and don't go below it. A tire that matches the size but not the load rating isn't a match.

## Step 3: Decide how much T-marking and foam matter to you

Tesla works with tire makers on tires built for its cars. According to Tesla's manual, most Tesla-approved tires carry a **Tx** marking on the sidewall (T0, T1, T2 and so on), and they're designed to reduce road noise and optimize handling, ride and range.

Tesla's tires page adds that it uses **acoustic foam** and optimized tread patterns in Tesla-designed tires to dampen road noise.

What that means when you shop:

- **Tesla's recommendation is a Tesla-approved tire.** If matching the original noise, ride and range feel is your priority, look for the T marking.
- **Foam affects repair.** A tire with foam bonded inside needs a shop that knows how to handle it. Mention it when you book a repair so the shop can plan for it.
- **Rolling resistance affects range.** Michelin notes EV tires are designed with low rolling resistance in mind to help range. If range is a priority, ask about it before you buy.

## Step 4: Weigh Florida's conditions, not a snow rating

Nobody in Broward is shopping for winter grip. What South Florida actually asks of a tire is wet braking in afternoon storms, heat, and long flat highway miles on the Turnpike and I-95.

A few tools help:

- **UTQG traction grade.** It reflects straight-line braking on wet pavement, graded AA, A, B or C. It doesn't cover hydroplaning or cornering, but it's a useful wet-braking signal. Our [UTQG guide](/learn/sidewall/utqg-ratings) explains all three grades.
- **Treadwear grade.** Useful for comparing tires within one brand, not across brands.
- **Tread depth over time.** Wet grip fades as tread wears. Our [tread depth chart](/learn/tread/tread-depth) explains the 2/32 and 4/32 lines.

## Step 5: Protect the new set with rotation and pairs

Once the new tires are on, two Tesla rules keep them lasting.

**Rotation.** Tesla's manual recommends rotating every **6,250 miles (10,000 km)**, or whenever the tread depth difference reaches **2/32 in (1.5 mm)** or more since the last rotation, whichever comes first. If your car has different front and rear sizes, rotation options are more limited, so ask your installer what applies to your setup.

**Replacing in pairs.** Tesla says to replace all four tires at once when a set is worn. If a tire has to be replaced early, say after a sidewall puncture, Tesla recommends replacing in pairs unless the other tires are within **2/32 in (1.5 mm)** of the new tire's tread depth.

**Flats.** Tesla offers an optional tire repair kit that can temporarily fix small punctures under 6 mm, enough to drive slowly to Tesla or a tire repair shop.
