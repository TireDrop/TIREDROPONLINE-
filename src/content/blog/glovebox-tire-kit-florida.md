## Why build the kit around the tires

Most car emergency kit lists are written for snow country: ice scrapers, blankets, cat litter for traction. In South Florida, the things that strand drivers look different. A flat from storm debris. A slow leak found on the way to the airport. An evacuation drive on a tire you meant to check last month.

So this kit starts with the general basics from Ready.gov and AAA, then adds the tire tools that actually get used here. Nothing on this list is a brand recommendation. Buy what fits your car and budget.

## The general basics

Ready.gov and AAA both publish car kit lists. The items that make sense year-round in Florida:

- **Phone charger** for the car
- **First-aid kit**
- **Flashlight** with fresh spare batteries
- **Jumper cables** or a portable jump pack
- **Reflective triangles or flares** to warn traffic
- **Basic tools,** plus duct tape
- **Rain poncho and gloves.** Tire trouble in an afternoon storm is common here.
- **Drinking water,** our own addition for the heat; swap it out every few months

## The tire kit

### 1. A tire pressure gauge

This is the one item NHTSA names directly: **keep a tire pressure gauge in your vehicle.** Its advice is to check all tires, **including the spare**, at least once a month when cold, meaning the car hasn't been driven for at least three hours. A simple dial or digital gauge is enough. Our guide to [checking tire pressure](/learn/pressure/how-to-check-tire-pressure) compares the types, and our post on [TPMS vs a gauge](/blog/tpms-still-need-tire-gauge) explains why the dash light isn't a substitute.

### 2. A 12-volt inflator

A small compressor that plugs into the car's power outlet lets you top up a slow leak and get to a shop, rather than driving on a soft tire. Many cars without a spare already carry one as part of a sealant kit; check before you buy a second.

### 3. Know what you have instead of a spare

AAA found **nearly one-third of new vehicles** were sold without a spare tire, with a sealant and inflator kit or run-flat tires in its place. Open the trunk floor today and find out which you have:

- **Compact spare:** check its pressure, which is usually higher than your regular tires and printed on its label.
- **Full-size spare:** check its pressure and its DOT date; it can be as old as the car.
- **Sealant kit:** check the expiration date (more below).
- **Run-flats:** nothing to carry, but know your car's distance and speed limits.

Our [spare tire types guide](/learn/damage/spare-tire-types) explains each one.

### 4. Know your sealant kit's limits

Sealant kits are for small punctures in the tread. The limits vary by car, which is why your own manual is the source to trust:

| Manual | Don't use the kit when | Speed after sealing |
|---|---|---|
| Tesla Model Y | Puncture larger than 1/4 inch (6 mm), severe tread damage, damaged sidewall | Drive within the limit shown on the kit, not at high speed or for long periods |
| Honda CR-V Hybrid (2025) | Puncture or cut larger than 3/16 inch (4 mm), damage to the sidewall or outside the contact area, more than one tire punctured | Do not exceed 50 mph |

Tesla's manual also says not to remove a foreign object, such as a nail, that's embedded in the tire before sealing. Both treat the kit as a temporary repair to reach a tire shop.

### 5. Check the expiration date

AAA's research found inflator kits typically have a **four- to eight-year shelf life**. If your car is a few years old and you've never looked, the sealant may already be out of date. The date is printed on the canister or bottle.

### 6. The small things that save a lot of time

- **The wheel lock key,** if your car has locking lug nuts. Keep it where you can find it.
- **The jack and lug wrench,** if your car has a spare. Find them now.
- **The owner's manual,** or a saved copy on your phone, open to the flat-tire section.
- **A piece of cardboard or a mat** to kneel on.

## The numbers to save

- **\*347** reaches the Florida Highway Patrol and, on covered highways, FDOT's Road Rangers. Our post on [what to do with a flat on I-95](/blog/flat-tire-on-i-95) explains what they do.
- **Your roadside assistance number,** from your insurer, your car's maker or a motor club.

## Storm season additions

FDEM's 2026 hurricane guidance says a good rule of thumb is to keep fuel tanks **at least halfway full** throughout hurricane season, and EV batteries **between 50 and 80 percent**, depending on the manufacturer's advice. Before a storm, run through our [hurricane car prep checklist](/blog/hurricane-season-tire-check), and after one, expect nails and debris on the roads; our post on [storm cleanup nails](/blog/storm-cleanup-nail-in-tire) covers what's repairable.

## Where to keep it, and when to check it

Keep the gauge in the glovebox or door pocket, where you'll actually reach for it. Everything else fits in a small bag in the trunk, next to the spare or the sealant kit so it's all in one place.

Then put two reminders in your phone: one in late May, before hurricane season starts on June 1, and one in November, before holiday travel. Each time, check the spare's pressure, the sealant's date, the flashlight batteries and the water.

## After you've used the kit

A sealed tire or a donut gets you to a shop. Tell the shop if you used sealant, and have the tire inspected from the inside.
