## The myth

Bigger wheels look great. Look at almost any performance car or upscale trim and the wheels get bigger as the price goes up, so it's natural to assume bigger wheels with thinner tires simply drive better.

They do drive *differently*. Whether that's better depends on what you want from the car, and on South Florida roads, the trade-offs are worth knowing before you spend money on a wheel upgrade.

## What "plus sizing" means

Plus sizing is moving to a larger wheel diameter while keeping the tire's overall height the same. To do that, the tire gets a lower **aspect ratio**, a shorter sidewall. For example, a car on 17-inch wheels going "plus one" moves to 18-inch wheels with a lower-profile tire, so the overall diameter barely changes.

That last part is the key rule. Bridgestone says replacement tires should always stay within **3 percent** of the original diameter, so features like anti-lock brakes and stability control keep working as designed. See how a size change moves the speedometer here:

## The part that's true: sharper steering

A shorter sidewall flexes less. Michelin describes it plainly: a taller sidewall absorbs road impacts more but flexes more in corners, while a lower-profile tire **feels sharper in steering response** but can be harsher over rough surfaces.

So if you want the car to react more crisply when you turn the wheel, plus sizing can deliver that. That's the kernel of truth in the myth.

## The trade-offs the myth leaves out

Bridgestone's own tire-size pages say that when the change is to larger diameter wheels, there may be trade-offs in the form of:

- **Increased ride harshness**
- **Longer braking distances**
- **Reduced fuel mileage**

Goodyear's advice on low-profile tires adds that steering feel, noise levels and driving comfort may all change. Here's how it adds up:

| Trait | Original size | Plus size (bigger wheel, lower profile) |
|---|---|---|
| Steering response | Softer | Sharper |
| Ride over rough roads | More cushioned | Harsher |
| Noise | Usually lower | May increase |
| Braking distance | Baseline | May be longer, per Bridgestone |
| Fuel economy or EV range | Baseline | May drop |
| Cost to replace | Baseline | Often higher |

## The EV example: same car, less range

Electric vehicles show the efficiency cost clearly, because the EPA rates range for each wheel option. On fueleconomy.gov, the **2026 Tesla Model Y Standard RWD** is rated:

- **18-inch wheels:** 138 MPGe, **321 miles** of range
- **19-inch wheels:** 131 MPGe, **303 miles** of range

Same car, same battery, 18 fewer miles of rated range from a one-inch step up in wheel size. Gas cars feel the same physics as reduced fuel mileage, just without a range number on the dash.

## Florida roads: potholes, curbs and less cushion

Less sidewall means less rubber between a pothole edge and your wheel. AAA's 2022 pothole survey found **1 in 10 drivers** had pothole damage bad enough to need a repair the year before, adding up to **26.5 billion dollars** in repairs nationwide in 2021.

Between construction zones, parking-garage curbs and flooded streets hiding holes, South Florida gives low-profile tires plenty to hit. If you go bigger, keep pressure at the placard and slow down for broken pavement. Our post on [hitting a curb or pothole](/blog/curb-pothole-tire-alignment-signs) covers the signs of damage afterward.

## If you still want bigger wheels, do it right

Plenty of drivers decide the look and the steering feel are worth it. That's a fair choice. Just make it carefully:

1. **Keep the overall diameter within 3 percent** of the original, as Bridgestone advises. Our [plus-size calculator](/plus-size-calculator) checks it for you.
2. **Never go below the original load rating.** Michelin notes that plus-sized tires aren't always interchangeable because of differences in load ratings, dimensions, clearance and rim size. Our guide to [load index](/learn/sidewall/load-index) shows how to read it.
3. **Match the speed rating** on your door placard or better.
4. **Get the wheel specs right:** bolt pattern, offset and center bore. Our guide to [wheel offset and backspacing](/learn/fitment/wheel-offset-backspacing) explains why it matters.
5. **Mind staggered setups.** Different sizes front and rear limit rotation; see our [staggered tires guide](/learn/fitment/staggered-tires).
6. **Check clearance** at full steering lock and with the suspension compressed.

## What else changes when you swap wheels

New wheels bring a few jobs people forget to budget time for:

- **TPMS sensors.** Nearly all the TPMS-equipped cars in NHTSA's field study used direct systems, with a sensor inside each wheel, usually on the valve stem. New wheels need those sensors moved over or new ones fitted, then the system relearned. Our [TPMS service](/services/tpms-service) handles both.
- **Lug nuts and torque.** Aftermarket wheels may need different lug nuts or hub rings, and every wheel should be torqued to the vehicle maker's spec.
- **Balance.** Every new wheel and tire pairing gets balanced. A vibration at highway speed after an upgrade usually points here first.
- **The spare.** Your spare won't match the new size. Check that it still clears the brakes and know its limits; our [spare tire types guide](/learn/damage/spare-tire-types) explains them.

## Try before you buy

If you're on the fence, ride in a friend's car with the larger wheel option, or ask the dealer to let you drive the same model on both sizes. Pay attention on the roughest street you drive every day, not the smoothest highway. Then check the range or fuel economy ratings for each wheel size, if your model lists them, and decide whether the look and the steering feel are worth the difference.

## The verdict

Bigger wheels don't always handle better. They steer sharper, and they usually ride harder, often cost more to replace and give up some mileage or range. For a daily driver on South Florida pavement, the original size is often the better all-round choice. If you want the upgrade anyway, keep the diameter and load rating right.
