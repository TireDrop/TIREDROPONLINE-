## The Jeep that mostly lives on pavement

Plenty of Wranglers in Broward never see a trail. They see I-95, school pickup lines, beach parking lots and, from May to October, a lot of afternoon rain. Tire marketing for Jeeps is all mud and rocks, but the right tire starts with where your miles actually happen.

We don't rank brands. This guide covers how to pick a tread type, what the owner's manual says about size, how bigger tires change things, and why the spare belongs in the conversation.

## Step 1: Be honest about your driving

Tire makers group Jeep tires into three main families. Our [all-terrain vs highway guide](/learn/buying/all-terrain-vs-highway-tires) goes deeper, but in short:

- **Highway (H/T).** Built for pavement. Bridgestone describes a smoother, quieter ride and efficient everyday performance.
- **All-terrain (A/T).** Deeper tread, wider grooves and larger blocks than a highway tire, for traction on dirt, gravel, grass and sand, usually with more road noise.
- **Mud-terrain (M/T).** Large blocks and wide voids that clear mud. Tire makers note the trade-off: more noise, less on-road grip and shorter tread life.

A quick way to choose:

| Your week | Tread type to look at first |
|---|---|
| Commuting, errands, highway trips | Highway |
| Mostly pavement, some dirt roads, boat ramps or sandy lots | All-terrain |
| Regular trail days in mud | Mud-terrain, with the on-road trade-offs |

Where beach driving is allowed at all, local rules come first, and many beaches don't allow it. If you air down for soft sand anywhere, follow the tire maker's guidance and put the placard pressure back before you're on pavement.

## Step 2: What the manual says about size

The Wrangler owner's manual tells you not to use a tire size, load rating or speed rating other than the one specified for your vehicle, and points you to the Tire and Loading Information placard on the driver's door for the details. It also warns that replacing the original tires with a **different size may result in false speedometer and odometer readings**.

That's the starting point for any Wrangler shopping list:

1. Read the size, load index and speed rating on your placard or current tires.
2. Decide on the tread type from Step 1.
3. Check whether the tire you like comes in a **P-metric or LT** version of your size. Many all-terrain and mud-terrain sizes are LT tires, which use load ranges and different pressures. Our [LT vs P-metric guide](/learn/buying/lt-vs-p-metric) explains what changes.

## Step 3: Bigger tires, with eyes open

"What's the biggest tire I can fit?" is the most-asked Jeep tire question, and the answer depends on the model, the trim and any lift. Before you commit:

- **Overall diameter moves the speedometer.** A taller tire makes it read slower than your true speed. Michelin says a change from the specified size should be made only with guidance from a trained tire professional.
- **Clearance matters at full steering lock and full suspension travel,** not just parked.
- **Load index can't drop.** A bigger tire still has to carry at least what the placard calls for.
- **The spare has to fit too.** A bigger set usually means a bigger spare and a check of the carrier.

## Step 4: Rain is the real test

South Florida's rainy season runs from mid-May to mid-October, and a Jeep's tall, boxy shape doesn't change the physics of standing water. Two things you control:

- **Tread depth, measured properly.** Big A/T and M/T blocks can look deep while the grooves that clear water are shallow. AAA's testing found tires worn to 4/32 of an inch took an average of 87 feet longer to stop on wet pavement than new ones, and AAA recommends replacing at 4/32.
- **Pressure, checked cold.** Use the placard number, or the load-and-inflation guidance from the tire maker for an LT size.

Our post on [why 4/32 matters in Florida rain](/blog/worn-tires-florida-rain-4-32) explains the numbers.

## Step 5: Put the spare in the rotation

Many Wranglers carry a spare that matches the other four. Mopar's own tire guide says that if you have a full-size spare, like on a Jeep Wrangler, you can include it in the rotation, and to check your owner's manual or ask a technician for the pattern. Mopar describes the rearward cross as its suggested method for most vehicles.

Five evenly worn tires mean the spare is a real match on the day you need it, and the wear is spread across five tires instead of four. Our [rotation pattern tool](/tire-rotation-pattern) shows the four-tire patterns; for five, follow your manual. While you're at it, register the new tires and check for recalls by vehicle and by tire on NHTSA's site.

## Mistakes we'd steer you away from

- **Buying a mud-terrain tire for a pavement Jeep.** You pay for it in noise, rain grip and tread life.
- **Going bigger without checking load and clearance.** Diameter is only one of the numbers.
- **Leaving the spare out of the plan.** A new set and an old spare don't match.
- **Trusting the look of the tread.** Measure in a main groove, not on a block edge.

## The Wrangler checklist

| Check | Where to find it | What to look for |
|---|---|---|
| Size, load and speed rating | Door placard, owner's manual | As specified, or a professionally confirmed alternative |
| Tread type | Your actual driving | Highway, all-terrain or mud-terrain |
| LT or P-metric | Tire sidewall | Load range and pressure that suit your use |
| Spare | Carrier and spare sidewall | Same size and type if it joins the rotation |
| Wet grip | Tread gauge, AAA | Replace at 4/32 |
