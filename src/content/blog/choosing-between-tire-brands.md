## The honest answer: it's the tire, not the logo

"Which tire brand is best?" is one of the most common questions in tire shopping. The honest answer is that it's the wrong unit of comparison.

Every major tire maker sells a range of lines: touring tires for quiet commutes, all-terrain tires for trucks, performance tires for grip, and value lines built to a price. Two tires with the same logo can be further apart than two tires from different makers built for the same job.

So instead of ranking brands (we don't), here's how to compare **tires**, using tools that work across any brand.

## Step 1: The placard comes before the brand

Before brand matters at all, a tire has to fit. USTMA's guidance is to match the size, load-carrying capacity and speed rating your vehicle maker specifies on the door placard or in the owner's manual.

- **Size** must match your placard, or an approved alternative in your manual.
- **Load index** must be at or above the original. Never go below it.
- **Speed rating** should be at or above the original.

That filter alone removes a lot of choices, and it's the same for every brand. Our [load and speed checker](/load-speed-check) compares any tire against your placard.

## Step 2: Pick the type for how you drive

Bridgestone's tire-type guide starts with your driving: mostly city and highway, performance, light off-road, or snow. In South Florida, snow drops off the list, which simplifies things.

| Type | Built for | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| All-season (touring or standard) | Dry and wet roads, comfort, tread life | Less ultimate grip than performance tires |
| Summer or performance | Grip and handling in warm weather | Often noisier, firmer and faster-wearing |
| All-terrain | Pavement plus dirt and sand | More noise, often more weight |
| Highway (truck and SUV) | Long pavement miles, comfort | Less off-road traction |

Choose the type first, then compare brands within it.

## Step 3: Use UTQG grades, and know their limits

Most passenger tires carry three Uniform Tire Quality Grading marks, required by NHTSA:

[[demo:utqg-explainer]]

- **Treadwear** is a number measured against a control tire graded 100. NHTSA's consumer guide notes the grades are assigned by the manufacturer based on its testing, so they're most useful for comparing tires **within one brand**, not across brands.
- **Traction** (AA, A, B or C) reflects straight-line braking on wet pavement. It doesn't measure cornering or hydroplaning.
- **Temperature** (A, B or C) reflects resistance to heat buildup at speed.

For a Florida driver, a higher traction grade is a useful signal for wet braking. Our [UTQG guide](/learn/sidewall/utqg-ratings) explains each grade in more detail.

## Step 4: Look at independent test results

UTQG is self-reported. Independent testing fills the gap. Consumer Reports, for example, buys tires and tests them for dry and wet braking, handling, **hydroplaning resistance**, noise, ride comfort, rolling resistance and tread life, using the same procedures for every brand.

When you read any test results, look at the categories that match your driving. A tire that wins in snow doesn't help much on I-95 in July. Wet braking and hydroplaning resistance matter most here.

## Step 5: Read the warranty, not just the number

Many tires carry a **treadwear warranty**: the maker's mileage promise, with conditions. Those conditions usually include regular rotation, correct pressure and records to prove it. Tire makers publish their own terms; Goodyear's tread life warranty page is a typical example of how they're structured.

A mileage warranty is easier to compare across brands than a UTQG treadwear grade. Just compare like with like: the same tire type, the same size range, and the conditions behind the number.

## What about premium vs budget?

Same-size tires can vary widely in what they cost. Our post on [why same-size tires cost different amounts](/blog/same-size-tires-different-prices) breaks it down: compound, construction, load rating, testing and warranty all play a part.

A practical way to decide:

- **High mileage or lots of rain driving?** Wet braking, hydroplaning resistance and tread life tend to matter most.
- **Low mileage?** Age may end the tire's life before tread does, so a long tread warranty matters less. See [how long tires last](/learn/tread/how-long-do-tires-last).
- **Any car:** the placard specification is non-negotiable, whatever the price.

## Marketing claims to read carefully

Tire marketing is full of words that sound like measurements. A few to slow down on:

- **"Best" or "number one" with no test named.** Ask: best at what, measured by whom?
- **A mileage number without the conditions.** Most treadwear warranties require rotation and records. Read the terms.
- **Comparisons across brands using treadwear grades.** NHTSA's guide notes the grade is assigned by each manufacturer, so it doesn't line up neatly between brands.
- **Snow and ice results.** Useful up north; much less relevant on I-95 in July.
- **"Fits your vehicle" without the load index.** A tire that matches the size but not the load rating isn't a match.

Plain criteria beat adjectives: the placard specification, the tire type, wet braking results, the warranty terms and the recall record.

## Two checks that apply to every brand

1. **Recalls.** NHTSA's recall site lists tire recalls by brand and line. After you buy, register your tires so the maker can reach you. Our guide to [tire recalls and registration](/learn/age/tire-recalls-registration) shows how.
2. **Matching.** Tire makers recommend a matched set, and many AWD vehicles require the same brand and tread pattern on all four. Our myth check on [replacing all four tires](/blog/replace-all-four-tires-myth) explains why.
