## Two numbers, two different jobs

Most drivers know one tread number: 2/32 of an inch, the point where the penny shows all of Lincoln's head. It's a real line. It's just not the line that matters most on a wet Broward highway in August.

Here's the short version. **2/32 marks the end of a tire's life. 4/32 marks the point where wet braking has already slipped a long way.** In a place where it rains hard most summer afternoons, the second number is the one to plan around.

## What 2/32 actually means

NHTSA's guidance is to replace a tire when the tread reaches 2/32 of an inch. Every tire has **wear bars**, small raised bridges molded into the bottom of the grooves. When the tread wears down to them and they sit flush with the surface, the tire is at 2/32 and done.

That's the line you shouldn't cross. But it describes a tire that has very little groove left to carry water away. It isn't a sign that the tire still grips well right up until that point.

## What AAA's wet-braking tests found at 4/32

In 2018, AAA worked with the Automobile Club of Southern California's Automotive Research Center to test new all-season tires against the same tires worn to **4/32 of an inch**, on wet pavement at highway speed.

The results, as AAA reported them:

| Measure | Worn to 4/32 vs new |
|---|---|
| Wet stopping distance, passenger car | 87 feet longer on average |
| Wet stopping distance, light truck | 86 feet longer on average |
| Increase in stopping distance | 43% on average |
| Handling ability, passenger car | 33% lower on average |

AAA's conclusion was to **replace tires at 4/32**, when stopping distances have already begun to get noticeably worse, rather than waiting for 2/32. Note what that means: the tires in the test still had twice the tread of the legal-wear line, and they still needed the length of several cars more to stop.

At least one vehicle maker draws the line in the same place. Tesla's Model Y owner's manual recommends replacing a tire when any tread depth reading is 4/32 in (3 mm) or less.

## Why this matters more in South Florida

Tread is a set of channels. On a wet road, the grooves give water somewhere to go so the rubber can stay in contact with the pavement. Shallower grooves move less water.

South Florida gives those grooves a lot of work:

- **The rainy season runs May 15 to October 15**, according to NWS Miami. Afternoon downpours are a normal part of the commute for five months.
- **Heavy rain arrives fast.** A dry road can carry standing water within minutes, before it drains.
- **Hydroplaning can start at modest speeds.** Bridgestone says it can happen at speeds as low as 35 mph, depending on the tires and the water.

Our post on [afternoon downpours on Broward roads](/blog/hydroplaning-broward-downpours) covers how to drive through it. This one is about the tires you bring into it.

## Measure, don't guess

You can't see 4/32 from the driver's seat. Measure it:

[[demo:tread-gauge]]

1. **Use a gauge if you can.** They're inexpensive and read in 32nds.
2. **Measure three grooves per tire**, inner, center and outer. Uneven wear often hides on the inside edge.
3. **Write down the lowest number** on each tire. That's the one that counts.
4. **Check all four.** Front and rear tires often wear at different rates.

No gauge? The quarter test gives a rough read: put a quarter upside down in a groove. If you can see all of Washington's head, you're at about 4/32 or less. Our [tread depth chart](/learn/tread/tread-depth) shows both lines in 32nds and millimeters, and our guide to [how to check tread depth](/learn/tread/how-to-check-tread-depth) covers the gauge, penny and quarter methods.

## What to do at each reading

| Lowest reading | What it means | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| 6/32 or more | Plenty of groove left | Keep rotating; recheck monthly |
| 5/32 | Getting close to AAA's line | Start shopping and plan the timing |
| 4/32 | AAA's replacement point | Replace, ideally before the next rainy season |
| 3/32 to 2/32 | At or near the wear bars | Replace now |
| Uneven across a tire | Alignment or pressure issue | Fix the cause when you replace the tires |

If you're at 5/32 in October, you probably have the winter. If you're at 5/32 in April, the rainy season will arrive before the tires are done. That's the whole case for planning around 4/32.

## When one part of the tire wears first

Tires rarely wear evenly by themselves. The lowest groove on a tire sets its replacement date, so uneven wear brings that date forward. Three patterns are worth knowing:

- **Both outer edges worn:** often a sign of running under-inflated. Check pressure monthly, cold, against the door placard.
- **Center worn:** can point to over-inflation, such as filling to the sidewall maximum instead of the placard number. Our post on the [sidewall max psi myth](/blog/sidewall-max-psi-myth) explains why that number isn't the target.
- **One edge worn:** usually alignment. A curb strike or a deep pothole can be enough to start it.

Rotating on schedule spreads normal wear across all four tires, and our [rotation pattern tool](/tire-rotation-pattern) shows the pattern for your drivetrain. If a new set starts wearing unevenly, fix the cause early. Otherwise one tire reaches 4/32 long before the rest.

## Tread isn't the only limit

A tire can be finished before the tread is. Age, sidewall cracking, bulges and repairs outside the tread area all count. Our guide on [how old is too old](/learn/age/how-old-is-too-old) explains the age guidance from tire makers, and our [repair checker](/can-my-tire-be-repaired) covers punctures.
