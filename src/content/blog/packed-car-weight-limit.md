## The trunk closes. That isn't the limit.

Five people, a week of luggage, two coolers and a roof box for the beach chairs. The trunk shuts, the doors close, and the car sits a little lower than usual. Is that too much?

Most drivers have no idea, because the number that answers it is one they've never read. It's on the driver's door, and it takes about two minutes to work out. Here's how, and what a heavy load means for your tires on a long, hot Florida drive.

## Where your weight limit lives

Open the driver's door and look at the edge of the door or the door post. You'll find the **Tire and Loading Information placard**, the same sticker that lists your tire size and cold tire pressure.

Under the federal placard standard (FMVSS No. 110), it carries one sentence that matters here:

> "The combined weight of occupants and cargo should never exceed" a stated number of pounds.

That number is your allowance for **everything you add to the empty car**: people, pets, luggage, coolers, the roof box, the bikes on the hitch. Some cars list it in kilograms too.

## The two-minute math

Federal rules (49 CFR 575.6) require owner's manuals for most cars and light trucks to include the same short set of steps for working out your real cargo allowance. In plain terms:

1. **Find the placard number**, the combined weight of occupants and cargo.
2. **Add up the weight** of the driver and every passenger riding along.
3. **Subtract** the people from the placard number.
4. **What's left is your cargo and luggage allowance.**
5. **Weigh what you're packing** against that allowance, and keep it under.
6. **Towing?** Load from a trailer is transferred to the vehicle, which shrinks the allowance further. Your manual explains how.

The worked example in the federal steps uses a placard limit of **1,400 lb** and **five passengers at 150 lb each**. That's 750 lb of people, leaving **650 lb** for cargo and luggage.

Run the numbers with your own placard and your own passengers. A full car of adults can use up most of the limit before the first bag goes in.

## What people forget to count

The placard number covers everything that isn't part of the car. These are the items that get left out of the math:

- **The roof box and what's in it.** The box itself counts, not just the contents. Your owner's manual may also list a separate roof load limit.
- **Hitch-mounted carriers and bikes.** They count, and they sit behind the rear axle.
- **Trailer tongue weight.** The downward push of the trailer on the hitch comes out of the same allowance.
- **Coolers.** Ice and drinks are heavy, and a beach day often means two of them.
- **Pets.** A large dog weighs as much as a small passenger.
- **Gear that lives in the car.** Tools, strollers and sports equipment you forgot were in the trunk.

| Item | Counts toward the placard limit? |
|---|---|
| Driver and passengers | Yes |
| Luggage, coolers and gear inside the car | Yes |
| Roof box or roof rack load | Yes, plus any roof limit in your manual |
| Hitch carrier and what's on it | Yes |
| Trailer tongue weight | Yes |

## Why the tires care

Every pound you add is carried by four small patches of rubber. NHTSA's consumer brochure, *Tire Safety: Everything Rides On It*, names three conditions that can cause **heat build-up and possible tire failure**: excessive speed, underinflation and **excessive loading**, separately or together.

A packed car on I-95 or the Turnpike in August can stack all three: a heavy load, sustained highway speed and, if nobody checked, tires a few pounds low. That's the combination to avoid. Our Learn guide on [Florida heat and tires](/learn/florida/florida-heat-tires) explains how heat, pressure and aging work together; this post sticks to the load.

## Pressure for a loaded car

Two rules cover it:

- **Set pressure cold, to the door placard,** before you load up and leave. Under the federal placard standard, that pressure is the vehicle maker's recommendation for the vehicle at its **maximum loaded weight**.
- **Check the owner's manual** for a second figure. Some manuals list a different pressure for a fully loaded car.

Never inflate past the maximum molded on the sidewall. That number is the tire's limit, not a target. USTMA's tire care guidance is to check pressure when the tires are cold, with a gauge, before the car has been driven.

## Replacement tires have to carry the load too

The placard limit assumes tires that can carry the weight the car was built for. That's why the load index on a replacement tire matters. USTMA's guidance for every vehicle is to match the placard's **size, load-carrying capacity and speed rating**.

- **Never go below the original load index.** Our [load index guide](/learn/sidewall/load-index) has the chart.
- **If the car came on XL tires, replace them with XL.** Our [XL vs SL guide](/learn/buying/xl-vs-sl-tires) explains why capacity depends on pressure.

## Hurricane evacuations, beach weekends and moving day

South Florida has its own versions of the overloaded car. An evacuation packs the family, the pets and the important papers into one vehicle. A Keys weekend means coolers and dive gear. Moving day means the car becomes a truck.

Do the placard math once, before you need it, and write your cargo allowance in your phone. Then:

- **For storm season,** our [hurricane season tire check](/blog/hurricane-season-tire-check) covers tread, pressure and the spare while the sky is clear.

## If the load is too much for your tires

If the math shows you'll be near the limit and your tires are worn, low on load index or not the size on the placard, sort that out before the trip.
