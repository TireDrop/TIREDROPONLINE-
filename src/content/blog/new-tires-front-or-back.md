## The answer most people don't expect

You need two tires. The fronts look worse, the fronts do the steering and, on most cars, the fronts put the power down. So the new pair goes on the front, right?

The tire industry says no. If you're replacing two tires, **the new ones go on the rear axle.** That holds on front-wheel-drive cars too. Here's who says so, why, and the cases where the rule works differently.

## Who says "rear", and how they put it

This isn't one shop's preference. The guidance lines up across the industry:

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| USTMA | If you can only replace two, install them on the rear axle. Newer tires have better grip, particularly on wet roads, and greater traction on the rear axle can better prevent a possible oversteer condition and loss of vehicle stability. |
| Tire Industry Association | When replacing only two tires, install the new tires on the rear. With new tires on the front and worn tires on the rear, the vehicle may be more likely to lose control when turning in wet or slippery conditions. |
| Michelin | Install two new tires on the rear axle. New tires give better wet grip than half-worn ones and help reduce the potential to fishtail and lose stability in the wet. |
| Bridgestone | If you replace just two tires, install them only on the rear axle, regardless of your vehicle's drivetrain. |

All four also say the same thing first: replacing all four at once is the recommendation. The rear-axle rule is for when two is the plan. Whether two is enough on your car is covered in [do you really need to replace all four tires?](/blog/replace-all-four-tires-myth)

## Why the rear: oversteer versus understeer

Picture a wet curve. Something has to give first, and which end gives decides what the car does.

**If the front tires lose grip first**, the car runs wider than you're steering. That's understeer. The car keeps heading roughly where it was going, just not as sharply as you asked.

**If the rear tires lose grip first**, the back of the car slides out. Bridgestone defines this as **oversteer**: the rear tires lose traction before the fronts, and the car turns more sharply than the driver intends. That's the fishtail Michelin describes, and Michelin notes that rear traction loss, particularly in the wet, is harder to recover from than front traction loss.

Deeper tread moves more water. Put the deeper tread on the rear and, in the wet, the rear is less likely to be the end that lets go first. Put it on the front and you've built a car that's more likely to slide its back end. That's the whole logic behind the rule.

## "But my car is front-wheel drive"

This is where the advice feels backwards, so it's worth slowing down on.

Front-wheel drive changes which wheels are pushed by the engine. It doesn't change which end of the car can slide in a wet corner. The rear tires still have to hold the back of the car in line, and worn rear tires still make oversteer more likely. That's why Bridgestone says the rear **regardless of drivetrain**.

What about the worn fronts? The new pair goes on the rear and the better of your old tires moves to the front. If the remaining tires are worn close to replacement, that's a sign the car may need four, not two.

## Rules that come with the rule

The rear-axle advice assumes a matched pair. Before mounting, check:

- **Size, load index and speed rating.** USTMA says replacement tires should be the same size, load index and speed rating your vehicle maker recommends.
- **Same size and type as the others.** Michelin says the new tires should match the size and tire type of the current ones.
- **Same category.** Bridgestone says to choose tires in the same category as your existing ones, so not two summer tires on a car running all-seasons.
- **The pair matches itself.** Same model on both sides of the axle.

## When the rule works differently

**Different sizes front and rear.** Some cars run wider tires on the rear than the front. Those tires can't swap axles, so each axle is replaced with its own size, and the front-or-back choice doesn't apply in the usual way. Follow the vehicle maker's guidance for that setup.

**All-wheel drive.** Many AWD vehicles have limits on how different the four tires can be, and some call for replacing all four. USTMA says to follow the vehicle maker's recommendations and restrictions, especially on all-wheel drive. Read the owner's manual before you buy two.

**One tire.** USTMA's advice for a single replacement is to pair it with the tire that has the deepest tread and install both on the rear axle, after checking the vehicle maker's rules.

## What it means in the rain

Wet roads are where this matters most, and where tread depth does its work. If you drive somewhere with frequent heavy rain, the case for deeper tread on the rear is the same case for replacing tires before they're worn all the way to 2/32 of an inch. Our [tread depth chart](/learn/tread/tread-depth) covers the 2/32 and 4/32 lines, and our post on [avoiding hydroplaning in Broward storms](/blog/hydroplaning-broward-downpours) covers driving habits in a downpour.

## After the install

The new pair won't stay on the rear forever. Rotation evens out wear across all four tires over time, which is how you avoid facing this choice again next year.

- **Rotate on your owner's manual's schedule.** Our [rotation pattern tool](/tire-rotation-pattern) shows the pattern for your drivetrain.
- **Check pressure monthly,** cold, against the door placard.
- **Measure tread** at each rotation, so you can see a gap growing before it's a problem.

## Quick checklist for two new tires

1. Confirm two is enough for your vehicle (owner's manual, especially on AWD).
2. Match size, load index and speed rating to the door placard.
3. Choose the same size, type and category as the tires staying on.
4. Tell the installer: **new pair on the rear axle.**
5. Move the better of the old tires to the front.
6. Start a rotation schedule.
