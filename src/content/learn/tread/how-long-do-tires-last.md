## The honest answer: until the first limit

There's no single number of miles or years for every tire. A tire is done when it hits **any one of three limits**:

| Limit | Where the line is | Who draws it |
|---|---|---|
| **Tread** | 2/32 inch to replace; 4/32 inch for wet roads | NHTSA; AAA |
| **Age** | Yearly inspection from 5 years; replace at 10 years | Bridgestone, Michelin |
| **Damage** | A bulge, a cut, or a puncture that can't be repaired | Your installer's inspection |

Whichever comes first is the one that counts. A low-mileage car can age out its tires with tread to spare. A commuter can wear through a set long before it's five years old.

## Miles: what decides how fast tread wears

Bridgestone gives a useful starting point. It notes that people drive **12,000 to 15,000 miles a year** on average, and that at that pace a good-quality all-season tire lasts **roughly three to five years**, depending on maintenance, driving style and conditions.

That range is wide for a reason. Goodyear's warranty terms list the things that affect tread life: tire type, tread compound, construction, the vehicle, maintenance, road and weather conditions, and driving habits. Some you choose when you buy. Others you control every month:

- **Pressure.** Run tires at the placard pressure, checked cold. Our guide on [how to check tire pressure](/learn/pressure/how-to-check-tire-pressure) walks through it.
- **Rotation.** Rotation moves each tire through the positions on the car on a schedule; see the [rotation pattern for your car](/tire-rotation-pattern).
- **Alignment.** Uneven wear across one tire is a clue to have it checked.
- **Load and driving habits.** Both are on Goodyear's list. Keep loads within what the door placard allows.

The only way to know where your tires stand is to measure. Our guide on [how to check tread depth](/learn/tread/how-to-check-tread-depth) shows the penny test, the quarter test and a gauge.

## Treadwear warranties vs UTQG grades

Two numbers on a tire listing look like they answer "how long will it last". Only one is a promise.

### A treadwear warranty is the maker's mileage promise

Many tires come with a **treadwear** (or tread life) **limited warranty**: a mileage figure the tire maker stands behind. Michelin, for example, covers its passenger and light truck replacement tires with a limited mileage warranty for treadwear.

Read the conditions, because they matter. Goodyear's tread life warranty terms, as one example, are **prorated**, apply to the original owner, ask for **rotation records**, and end when the tire wears to its wear bars (2/32) or after a set number of years from purchase, whichever comes first. Other makers' terms differ, so check the warranty for the exact tire.

What a warranty tells you: how long the maker expects the tread to last under normal use with proper care. What it doesn't: that your tires will reach that number.

### UTQG treadwear is a comparison, not miles

The **UTQG treadwear grade** on the sidewall, like the 500 in "500 AA A", is something else. Under the federal grading rule, it compares a tire's wear with a control tire graded 100 on a government test course. The tire maker assigns it, and NHTSA's consumer guide warns that real-world results can depart significantly from the norm because of driving habits, service practices, roads and climate.

There's no formula that turns a grade into miles. Use it to compare tires within one brand; use the warranty to judge mileage. Our [UTQG guide](/learn/sidewall/utqg-ratings) breaks down all three grades.

## Years: the age limit, whatever the tread

Tires age even when they aren't wearing. NHTSA's TireWise guidance notes that some vehicle and tire makers recommend replacing tires **6 to 10 years old, regardless of treadwear**. Bridgestone and Michelin set the same two markers:

- **From 5 years:** a professional inspection at least once a year.
- **At 10 years:** replace the tire, even if it looks good. Bridgestone's guidance includes the spare.

A tire's age starts at its DOT date code, not the day it went on your car. Your vehicle maker may set a shorter timeframe in the owner's manual. Our guide [How Old Is Too Old for Tires?](/learn/age/how-old-is-too-old) lays out the guidance side by side, and [reading the tire date code](/learn/sidewall/dot-date-code) shows where to find the date.

## The Florida factors

South Florida is hard on tires in ways a mileage number doesn't show.

**Heat.** NHTSA lists excessive speed, underinflation and overloading as conditions that can cause heat build-up and possible tire failure, and it calls underinflation the leading cause of tire failure. Summer heat adds to that, so the monthly cold pressure check matters more here, not less. Our guide to [Florida heat and tires](/learn/florida/florida-heat-tires) covers it in detail.

**Sun and aging.** NHTSA lists sunlight and a warmer climate among the things that contribute to tire aging, and its research found aging is a greater concern in the southern part of the Sun Belt. Take the five-year inspection seriously.

**Rain.** AAA's testing found tires worn to 4/32 took an average of 87 feet longer to stop on wet roads at 60 mph than new tires. If you drive through the rainy season, 4/32 is the practical end of a tire's life, not 2/32.

**Cars that sit.** A second car or a seasonal car can reach five years with most of its tread left. Low miles don't mean young tires.

## How to make tires last their full life

1. **Check pressure monthly**, cold, against the door placard.
2. **Measure tread monthly** in three grooves on every tire.
3. **Rotate on schedule** and keep the records if your tires have a tread warranty.
4. **Watch for uneven wear** and have alignment checked if you see it.
5. **Read the DOT dates** once a year, spare included.
6. **Don't overload** past what the placard allows.
