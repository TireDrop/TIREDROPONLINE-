## The short answer

"Plug or patch?" sounds like a choice between two repairs. Under industry guidance it isn't. **Neither one on its own is an accepted repair.** A proper repair uses both at once, from the inside of the tire, after the tire has been taken off the wheel and inspected.

Here's what each piece does, the limits on where a tire can be repaired, and what to ask for when you have a nail in the tread.

## What a plug does, and what it doesn't

A plug is a cord of sticky rubber pushed into the puncture from the outside. AAA describes the process: the hole is reamed to clean it, then the plug is threaded through and pushed deep into it.

A plug fills the hole. What it doesn't do is seal the inner liner, the airtight layer on the inside of the tire. The Tire Industry Association puts it plainly: **a plug by itself does not permanently seal the innerliner.** USTMA's procedures call a plug alone an **unacceptable repair.**

## What a patch does, and what it doesn't

A patch is applied to the inside of the tire, over the inner liner. It seals the air in.

What it doesn't do is fill the channel the nail or screw made through the tread and belts. TIA's explanation: **a patch by itself does not fill the void left by the penetrating object, which allows water to enter the body of the tire.**

## The repair the industry accepts: both, from the inside

USTMA's guidance for passenger and light truck tires is that a **rubber stem, or plug, must fill the puncture injury and a patch must seal the inner liner.** Only this plug and patch combination is considered permanent.

## Where a tire can be repaired, and where it can't

USTMA and TIA draw the repairable zone tightly:

- **Tread area only.** USTMA recommends considering a repair only when the damage is limited to the tread area.
- **1/4 inch (6 mm) or smaller.** The puncture can't be larger than 1/4 inch in diameter.
- **Not the sidewall or shoulder.** USTMA says punctures or cuts there can't be repaired, because that part of the tire flexes the most and lacks the tread area's reinforcement.
- **Not at the edge, or at an angle into it.** TIA adds that a repair can't be made where the injury extends into the shoulder or belt edge area, or runs at an angle into the shoulder.
- **No overlapping repairs.** A new repair can't overlap an old one.

| Where the damage is | Size | Under USTMA/TIA guidance |
|---|---|---|
| Tread area | 1/4 in (6 mm) or smaller | May be repairable, after the inside is inspected |
| Tread area | Larger than 1/4 in | Not repairable; replace |
| Shoulder or belt edge, or angled into the shoulder | Any | Not repairable; replace |
| Sidewall | Any | Not repairable; replace |
| Overlapping an earlier repair | Any | Not repairable; replace |

Some tire makers set their own limits on top of these, for run-flat tires and other types. USTMA notes this, so a technician will follow the tire maker's rules where they're stricter.

## Why the tire has to come off the wheel

USTMA is firm on this: the tire must be **removed from the rim or wheel** and inspected for all possible damage, including damage to the inner liner. Its procedures say **never** perform an outside-in repair or an on-the-wheel repair.

The reason is simple: damage to the inner liner can't be seen from the tread side. The only way to find it is to look inside.

## About roadside plug kits and sealant

Plug kits and cans of sealant are sold for roadside use, and some cars ship with a sealant kit instead of a spare. Whatever they do on the shoulder, they aren't the repair the industry guidance describes.

One rule is worth knowing before you use a plug kit. USTMA's procedures say **never repair a tire that has an existing, improper repair; the tire must be scrapped.** A plug on its own is an improper repair under that same guidance. If you've plugged a tire yourself, tell the technician, and expect them to judge the tire with that in mind.
