## The short answer

Both all-season and all-weather tires are built to stay on the car twelve months a year. The difference is one symbol on the sidewall.

- **All-season tires** usually carry **M+S** only. Michelin describes them as built for everyday driving, with balanced dry and wet performance and acceptable snow traction where winters are light.
- **All-weather tires** carry the **three-peak mountain snowflake** (often shortened to 3PMSF), usually alongside M+S. That symbol means the tire met a measured snow traction standard.

So the real question isn't "all-season or all-weather?" It's "do I need tested snow traction where this car actually goes?" In Sunrise or Kendall, the honest answer is usually no. In Asheville in January, it's a different conversation.

## What M+S actually means

M+S stands for mud and snow. It's one of the oldest markings on a tire, and it's widely misunderstood.

Continental's explanation is blunt: manufacturers use M+S to show a tire is meant for winter conditions, but the performance of M+S tires is "neither defined nor measured." Michelin makes the same point: the M+S marking is based on tread geometry, not a certified performance test.

In practice, M+S tells you the tread pattern has the kind of grooves and edges associated with mud and snow use. It doesn't tell you how the tire performed on snow, because no snow test is required to put it on the sidewall.

That's why almost every all-season tire carries M+S. It's a description of the tread, not a grade.

## What the three-peak mountain snowflake means

The 3PMSF symbol is different because it's earned in a test.

The U.S. Tire Manufacturers Association (USTMA) and the Rubber Association of Canada agreed on a performance-based definition for passenger and light truck tires for use in severe snow conditions. USTMA's bulletin on it, TISB 37, sets the bar like this:

- the tire is tested on **packed snow** under an ASTM test method,
- it's compared against a **standard reference test tire** rated 100,
- and it has to reach a **traction index of at least 110** against that reference (112 when the 16-inch reference tire is used).

A tire that meets the standard can carry the three-peak mountain snowflake pictograph. Continental notes that the same symbol is used under UNECE rules in Europe and other markets, as well as in the U.S. and Canada.

### What the snow test doesn't cover

Here's the catch that matters for buyers. Michelin describes the qualifying test as a **traction test on medium-packed snow**, and notes that the symbol doesn't cover performance on **pure ice**.

So the snowflake tells you a tire cleared a real bar in one specific condition. It tells you nothing about:

- grip on ice,
- grip on wet roads,
- tread life,
- road noise or ride comfort,
- how the tire behaves in heat.

For those, you still need the tire maker's own spec page, its treadwear warranty, and the UTQG grades molded on the sidewall. Our guide to [UTQG ratings](/learn/sidewall/utqg-ratings) explains what those three grades can and can't tell you.

## Side by side

| Feature | All-season | All-weather | Winter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical sidewall marking | M+S | 3PMSF, usually with M+S | 3PMSF, usually with M+S |
| Snow traction tested to a standard? | No | Yes (packed snow) | Yes (packed snow) |
| Meant to stay on all year? | Yes | Yes | No, swapped seasonally |
| Where makers aim it | Light winters, mostly rain and dry roads | Real winters without a second set of tires | Cold, snow, slush and ice |

Michelin describes winter tires as purpose-built for cold, ice, snow and slush, and all-weather tires as a way to get tested snow capability without switching sets each season.

## How to choose for where you drive

### If the car lives in South Florida

Snow traction almost never comes into it here. What does come into it is rain: afternoon downpours on I-95, standing water on the Sawgrass, and hot pavement the rest of the time.

Put your attention on:

1. **The size, load index and speed rating on your door placard.** These come first, whatever the tire type. If you're not sure what yours are, our guide on [how to read tire size](/learn/sidewall/how-to-read-tire-size) walks through each piece.
2. **Wet performance claims on the maker's spec page.** Read what the maker itself says about the tire.
3. **Tread life.** Compare the treadwear warranty and the UTQG treadwear grade within the same brand.
4. **Heat and age.** Florida heat ages rubber. Our [Florida heat guide](/learn/florida/florida-heat-tires) covers what that means for the tires you choose.

An all-weather tire isn't wrong for Florida. It just carries a capability you'll rarely use here, so weigh it against the things you will.

### If you drive north in winter, or the car lives somewhere with snow

TireDrop ships free to the 48 contiguous states and DC, so plenty of our buyers do see snow. If yours does, the snowflake becomes a real filter:

- **Occasional snow, no second set of tires:** all-weather tires with the 3PMSF symbol are what makers aim at this driver.
- **Regular snow, ice and deep cold:** makers point you to dedicated winter tires, swapped out in spring.
- **Mixed fleet or a winter road trip:** check whether any state or mountain pass on your route has chain or traction-device rules before you go.

### Don't mix types across the car

Michelin recommends the same tire model on all four positions where you can, and matching pairs on each axle at a minimum. Mixing an all-weather pair with an all-season pair gives the front and rear axles different grip in the very conditions where you want them to match.

## Reading the sidewall in the store, or in the photo

When you're comparing tires online, look for these in the product photos or the spec table:

- **M+S** (sometimes M/S or M&S) molded near the size.
- **The three-peak mountain snowflake**, a small mountain outline with a snowflake inside it.
- **The full size, load index and speed symbol**, for example 225/65R17 102H.

If the snowflake isn't listed in the specs and you can't see it in a photo, don't assume it's there. Ask before you buy.
