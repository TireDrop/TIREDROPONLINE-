## Same tire size, different job

An electric car rolls on the same kind of tire sizes as a gas car. What it asks of those tires is different.

The battery makes an EV heavy for its size. The motor delivers its pull instantly. The cabin is quiet enough that you hear the road. And every bit of energy the tires waste comes straight out of the battery. Michelin's EV guidance lists those same pressures: **weight, torque, noise and range**.

That's why EV tires exist as a category. Here's what each difference means when you're buying.

## Weight: load index, XL and HL

Battery packs add weight without making the car any bigger. Michelin explains that EVs are heavier because of their batteries and so need tires with **higher load indexes**.

You'll see three load classes on passenger tires:

- **SL (standard load)**, the default.
- **XL (extra load)**, which carries more at a higher pressure. Our [XL vs SL guide](/learn/buying/xl-vs-sl-tires) explains how it works.
- **HL (high load)**, the newest. Michelin describes HL as a standard developed for the **heaviest EVs and large hybrid SUVs**, carrying more load than an XL tire of the same size at the same pressure.

Pirelli says the **HL marking is printed in front of the size**, as in HL 265/35R21, and that an HL tire carries about 6% to 9% more than an XL tire of the same size.

The rule is simple. Michelin says that if your EV came from the factory on HL tires, **every replacement must also be HL**. The same goes for load index: match or exceed what the door placard says. The [load and speed checker](/load-speed-check) compares two tires side by side.

## Noise: why many EV tires have foam inside

With no engine noise to cover it, the sound of tread on pavement is what you hear. Michelin notes that because EVs make less noise, **tire and road noise is more noticeable**, and that tires tuned for EVs pair quieter tread designs with noise-reduction technology such as **Michelin Acoustic**.

The common fix is a layer of **sound-absorbing foam** bonded inside the tire. Makers sell it under their own names: Continental's ContiSilent, Pirelli's Noise Cancelling System (PNCS) and Michelin Acoustic. Tesla says it uses acoustic foam and tuned tread patterns in Tesla-designed tires.

One practical point: foam changes how a puncture is repaired. Continental says a ContiSilent tire must be inspected by a trained specialist and repaired with an approved method, with part of the foam removed around the puncture. Tell the shop before a repair. Our guide to [Tesla-marked and acoustic tires](/learn/tesla/tesla-t-marked-acoustic-tires) goes deeper.

## Range: rolling resistance

A rolling tire flexes, and that flexing turns some energy into heat. Michelin calls it **hysteresis** and says it can't be avoided, only minimized. Tires engineered for lower rolling resistance help extend an EV's range.

What you control is pressure. Michelin says every psi below your vehicle's recommended pressure **increases rolling resistance and reduces range**, and that tires lose about 1 psi a month. Check pressure cold, monthly, against the door placard.

## Torque and wear

An electric motor delivers full pulling power from a standstill. Michelin says EV tire wear comes largely from **battery weight and instant torque**, and puts the average at about **20% faster wear** than a comparable gas car.

Michelin's ways to slow it down:

- **Keep pressure correct.**
- **Rotate regularly** so all four wear evenly. The [rotation pattern tool](/tire-rotation-pattern) shows the pattern for your setup; your owner's manual's schedule comes first.
- **Accelerate smoothly.** Hard launches spike torque and scrub rubber off the driven tires.
- **Choose tires with compounds built for EV use.**

## Carmaker marks: T0, T1 and others

Some sidewalls carry a short code showing the tire was developed for a specific carmaker. Pirelli lists examples: **T0 for Tesla, N0 for Porsche, MO for Mercedes and AO for Audi**.

For Tesla, the owner's manuals say a Tesla-approved tire carries a **T mark** such as T0, T1 or T2. Tesla's tires page says T0 is the first version of a Tesla-designed tire, and later versions with new tuning are marked T1, T2 and so on.

A tire without the mark can still fit, as long as it matches the size, load index (including XL or HL) and speed rating. What changes is the tuning: noise, ride, handling and range can differ. Our [Tesla tire basics](/learn/tesla/tesla-tire-basics) and [Tesla tire sizes](/learn/tesla/tesla-tire-sizes) guides cover each model, and the [Model Y tire guide](/blog/tesla-model-y-tires-guide) is on the blog.

## Do you have to buy an "EV tire"?

No. What you have to match is the specification on the door placard: **size, load index (including any XL or HL marking) and speed rating**. A tire that meets all three can go on an EV.

What an EV-specific tire adds is tuning for the four pressures above: compounds built for weight and torque, low rolling resistance for range, and often foam for a quieter cabin. A standard all-season in the right size and ratings can do the job, but it may be louder, wear differently or cost you some range. Decide which of those trade-offs matter to you, then compare tires on them.

## No spare in the trunk

Many EVs carry no spare. Teslas generally don't come with one; Tesla sells an optional repair kit. Our guide to [spare tire types](/learn/damage/spare-tire-types) explains the options and their limits.
