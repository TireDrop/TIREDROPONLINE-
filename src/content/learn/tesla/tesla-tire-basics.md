## Why a Tesla is hard on tires

A tire on a Tesla does the same basic job as a tire on any car: it carries the weight, grips the road and soaks up bumps. What changes is how much of each it has to do.

Michelin sums up the difference in a few points. EVs are **heavier** than comparable gas vehicles, they deliver **instant torque**, they use **regenerative braking** to recharge on deceleration, and they're **quiet**, so tire noise is easier to hear. On top of that, carmakers want tires with **low rolling resistance** to stretch range.

Each of those pulls a tire in a slightly different direction. Here's what each one means for you.

## Weight: the load rating matters more

Battery packs are heavy. Hankook says battery weight can make an EV up to 30% heavier than a comparable gas vehicle. Michelin puts it simply: vehicle weight is a critical factor in how fast a tire wears, and more weight means faster wear.

Weight also decides which tires can go on the car at all. Every tire has a **load index**, the number after the size, that says how much weight it's built to carry at its maximum pressure. Some EV tires also carry an **XL** (extra load) or **HL** (high load) marking. Michelin explains that an HL tire carries more load than an XL tire of the same size at the same inflation pressure.

The rule for replacements is the same on every car: match or exceed the load index your vehicle was specified with. Tesla's manual says to use tires that match the original specification. You'll find yours on the Tire and Loading Information placard on the door pillar and in the owner's manual.

## Instant torque and regenerative braking

An electric motor delivers its torque the moment you press the pedal. Michelin points out that this is what gives EVs their quick acceleration, and that EVs combine it with regenerative braking, which slows the car by turning the motor into a generator.

For the tread, that means force going through the contact patch both when you speed up and when you lift off. Continental describes the result plainly: tires on EVs carry a heavier load and have to withstand high instant torque, which leads to higher wear, so they need stronger construction and tougher compounds.

How you drive changes how much of that force the tread sees. Frequent hard launches put more of it through the tires than smooth starts do. Uneven wear between positions is one reason Tesla publishes a specific rotation interval (more on that below).

## Low rolling resistance: range has a price

**Rolling resistance** is the energy a tire uses up as it flexes and rolls. Lower rolling resistance means more of the battery goes to moving the car, which is why EV makers ask for it. Continental says the right tire can help battery range, and Hankook says EV-specific tires are designed to minimize rolling resistance.

The catch is that rolling resistance, wet grip and tread life pull against each other. Michelin is direct about it: carmakers often prioritize range when optimizing original-equipment tires for an EV, and that comes with trade-offs such as less grip or greater tire wear.

That doesn't make a low rolling resistance tire a bad choice. It means you should know what a tire was optimized for before you buy it. If most of your driving is wet South Florida highway, wet grip may matter more to you than the last few miles of range. Our guide to [Tesla tire types for South Florida](/learn/tesla/tesla-tires-south-florida) walks through that trade-off.

## Noise: why you hear your tires more

A gas engine hides a lot of road noise. Take it away and the tires become one of the loudest things you hear. Michelin notes that because EVs make less noise, the sound of the tire meeting the road is more intrusive.

Tire makers tackle this two ways: tread patterns tuned to be quieter, and **sound-absorbing foam** bonded inside the tire. Tesla says it uses acoustic foam and optimized tread patterns in Tesla-designed tires to dampen road noise. Michelin, Continental, Pirelli and Goodyear all make their own versions.

Foam changes a few things when a tire needs a repair, so it's worth knowing whether yours has it. Our guide to [Tesla-marked and foam-lined tires](/learn/tesla/tesla-t-marked-acoustic-tires) covers what to look for.

## Why EV tires wear faster, and what helps

Put the pieces together and you get the headline number. Michelin says that, on average, EVs wear out a tire **about 20% faster** than a comparable gas vehicle. Hankook gives the same figure and adds that the wear is often uneven on tires that weren't designed for EVs.

Tire makers are responding. Michelin says its newer tire lines are engineered with more wear potential to offset that 20%, and Hankook describes EV tires built to spread contact pressure more evenly.

Whatever tire you run, the habits that stretch tread life are the same ones USTMA recommends for every car:

- **Pressure.** Check it monthly with a gauge, with the tires cold, against the door placard. Tesla's manual says not to depend on the tire pressure monitoring system to judge pressures: keeping them correct is the driver's job.
- **Rotation.** Tesla's manual gives a specific interval for its cars. See [Tesla tire rotation, alignment and TPMS](/learn/tesla/tesla-rotation-alignment-tpms).
- **Alignment.** Uneven wear on one edge, or a car that pulls, are signs to have it checked.
- **Tread depth.** Measure several grooves on every tire. Our [tread depth chart](/learn/tread/tread-depth) explains the 2/32 and 4/32 lines.

## What to check when you replace Tesla tires

| What to check | Where to find it | Why it matters on a Tesla |
|---|---|---|
| Size, front and rear | Door placard, owner's manual | Some Tesla wheel packages use wider rear tires |
| Load index, XL or HL | Owner's manual, current tire sidewall | Battery weight; never go below the specification |
| Speed rating | Owner's manual, current tire sidewall | Match or exceed the original |
| T marking | Tire sidewall | Tesla's recommended choice, tuned for its cars |
| Acoustic foam | Tire maker's product page | Quieter cabin; affects how a repair is done |
| Rolling resistance | Tire maker's product page | Range versus grip and tread life |
