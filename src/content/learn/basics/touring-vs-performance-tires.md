## Two tires, two jobs

Every tire is a compromise. Make the rubber softer and it grips harder but wears faster. Stiffen the sidewall and the steering sharpens but the ride firms up. Touring and performance tires are two ends of that trade.

Continental's definitions are a good starting point:

- **Touring tires** are built for long-distance driving, offering **comfort, low noise and durability.**
- **Performance (sport) tires** have tread patterns designed for **strong traction in wet and dry conditions.**

Both are road tires for cars, crossovers and SUVs. Neither is "better". The right one depends on the car and how you drive it.

## What changes inside the tire

Tire makers describe the same set of trade-offs:

| Feature | Touring | Performance |
|---|---|---|
| Rubber compound | Harder-wearing, built for mileage | Softer, built for grip |
| Sidewall | More flex, for ride comfort | Stiffer, to resist flex in corners |
| Ride and noise | Smoother and quieter | Firmer, with more road noise |
| Tread life | Longer | Shorter |
| Speed ratings | Varies; read the sidewall | H and V for performance touring; W, Y and Z for ultra-high performance (BFGoodrich) |

Michelin explains the performance side: **stiffer sidewalls that resist flex in corners**, and a **softer compound** that makes the tire feel sharp but **wears faster, particularly with aggressive driving.** The cost, in Michelin's words, is **a firmer ride, more road noise, and faster tread wear compared to touring or all-season tires.** BFGoodrich lists the same price: faster tread wear, typically more road noise and a firmer ride.

## Reading the category off the sidewall

Marketing names like "grand touring" or "max performance" aren't regulated, and every brand draws the lines a little differently. Two markings on the sidewall tell you more.

### The speed rating

BFGoodrich groups tires by their speed symbol:

- **H and V** ratings are **typically considered "performance touring."**
- **W, Y and Z** ratings, **with an aspect ratio of less than 55**, are **ultra-high performance** sport tires, typically for wheels 16 inches and larger, made to sharpen handling.

A speed rating is a lab test result, not a suggested driving speed. Michelin defines it as the maximum speed at which a tire can carry its maximum load under specified conditions. Our [speed rating guide](/learn/sidewall/speed-rating) has the full chart.

### The UTQG treadwear grade

Most passenger tires carry a three-part UTQG grade, such as **500 AA A**, under a federal rule (49 CFR 575.104). The first number is treadwear, measured against a control tire graded 100.

Michelin gives a sense of the spread: **high performance summer tires commonly fall in the 200 to 300 range; touring tires can reach 700 or higher.** That gap is the compound trade-off in a single number.

## Touring tires: who they suit

Touring tires fit most daily drivers: commuters, family crossovers, ride-share cars and anyone who'd rather hear the radio than the road. Many are sold as all-season tires, with long tread life and a quiet, compliant ride as the selling points.

What you give up is some steering sharpness and ultimate grip, which most drivers on I-95 at rush hour never use.

## Performance tires: who they suit

Performance tires make sense when the car was built around them. Michelin's point is direct: **a vehicle that came equipped with a V, W, or Y speed-rated tire was engineered around performance-grade rubber.** If your door placard or original tires show one of those ratings, the car was set up with performance tires in mind.

They come in two broad flavors:

- **Performance all-season**, which trades a little grip for a longer season and more tread life.
- **Summer (max or ultra-high performance)**, which gives the most grip. Michelin notes **UHP summer tires use a compound built for temperatures above 45°F.**

That 45°F line rarely matters in South Florida, where summer tires are a realistic year-round choice for drivers who want them. The trade is tread life and noise, not climate.

## Switching from one to the other

You can move between categories, within limits:

1. **Start at the door placard.** The replacement must **meet or exceed** the vehicle maker's speed rating, Michelin says, and the load index has to be equal or higher too. Check any candidate with the [load and speed rating check](/load-speed-check).
2. **Going from performance to touring** usually means a quieter ride and more miles, as long as the speed rating still meets the placard. Michelin's advice for sport and luxury cars is to match or exceed the original speed rating and performance category to keep the driving experience.
3. **Going from touring to performance** sharpens the steering and shortens tread life. Expect more noise.
4. **Keep all four the same.** Pirelli's advice is that it's always best to avoid mixing tires with different characteristics, including speed rating.

## Quick reference

| You want | Look for |
|---|---|
| Quiet ride, long tread life | Touring all-season; higher treadwear grade within the brand |
| Sharper handling, more grip | Performance all-season or summer; H, V or higher speed rating |
| Maximum grip, warm climate | Summer UHP; W, Y or Z rating; lower treadwear grade |
| The car's original character | Match or exceed the original speed rating and category |
