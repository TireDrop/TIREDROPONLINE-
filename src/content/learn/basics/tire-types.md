## Three questions sort every tire

Tire listings throw a lot of labels at you: all-season, grand touring, A/T, UHP, run-flat, directional. They make more sense once you see they answer three different questions:

1. **What climate is it built for?** Summer, winter, all-season or all-weather.
2. **What kind of driving is it built for?** Touring, performance or all-terrain, plus light truck construction for heavy loads.
3. **How does it handle a puncture?** A regular tire or a run-flat.

A single tire answers all three. A "touring all-season" is one climate type and one driving type. Here's what each label means, in the tire makers' own terms.

## By climate: summer, winter, all-season, all-weather

Michelin draws the line at **45°F**:

| Type | Michelin's description | Built for |
|---|---|---|
| Summer | **Excellent cornering and braking above 45°F**, on warm, dry or wet roads | Warm climates and performance cars |
| Winter | **Stay flexible below 45°F**, for snow, slush, ice and freezing rain | Real winters |
| All-season | **Work year-round in moderate climates**; good in rain and light snow, not heavy snow or ice | Most cars, most places |
| All-weather | Carry the 3PMSF snowflake for certified cold-weather performance in one set | Year-round use where winters bring some snow |

Continental describes summer tires as having **a dedicated rubber compound that delivers excellent grip and handling on both dry and wet roads in warmer conditions**, and all-season tires as **a hybrid** of summer and winter characteristics.

Two sidewall marks tell climate types apart. Michelin explains that **M+S** means the tire is designed for mud and snow **based on the manufacturer's criteria**, while the **three-peak mountain snowflake (3PMSF)** means it **met standardized testing requirements** for verified winter performance. Our guide to [all-season vs all-weather tires](/learn/buying/all-season-vs-all-weather-tires) goes deeper on that difference.

**In South Florida,** the realistic choice is between all-season, all-weather and summer tires. Snow isn't the deciding factor; wet grip, heat and tread life are.

## By the kind of driving: touring, performance, all-terrain

Continental's definitions:

- **Touring tires** are **built for long-distance driving, offering comfort, low noise, and durability.**
- **Performance (sport) tires** have tread patterns aimed at **strong traction in wet and dry conditions.** Michelin notes their **stiffer sidewalls** and softer compound, which **wears faster**.
- **All-terrain tires** are **a good match for off-road use on larger SUVs and trucks**, with aggressive tread patterns.

Touring and performance tires are the main split for cars and crossovers; see [touring vs performance tires](/learn/basics/touring-vs-performance-tires). For pickups and SUVs, the split is highway vs all-terrain vs mud-terrain, covered in [all-terrain vs highway tires](/learn/buying/all-terrain-vs-highway-tires).

### Light truck (LT) and passenger tires

Construction is its own type. The federal tire standard defines an **LT tire** as one **designated by its manufacturer as primarily intended for use on lightweight trucks or multipurpose passenger vehicles**, and requires its load range letter on the sidewall. Many all-terrain sizes are LT tires, which changes load capacity and inflation pressures. Our [LT vs P-metric guide](/learn/buying/lt-vs-p-metric) covers when a truck needs LT.

## By puncture handling: regular or run-flat

Most tires lose their ability to carry the car once they lose air. A **run-flat** tire is built to keep going for a limited distance after a puncture. Continental describes it as remaining operational until the driver can reach home or the nearest garage. Run-flats carry their own speed and distance limits from each maker, and they need a working TPMS because they don't look flat. See [run-flat tires](/learn/buying/run-flat-tires) for those limits.

## By tread pattern: symmetrical, asymmetric, directional

Inside any category, the tread itself comes in three layouts. Continental describes them:

| Pattern | What it looks like | What Continental says it's good at | Mounting |
|---|---|---|---|
| Symmetrical | **Both halves of the tire feature the same pattern** | Smooth driving, directional stability, low rolling resistance | Any way round |
| Asymmetric | **Two separate tread designs**, one on the inner half and one on the outer | Handling, curve stability, wet grip | Marked side faces out |
| Directional | **Lateral grooves that meet in the middle**, like an arrowhead | Resisting aquaplaning, high-speed road holding | Must roll the way the **sidewall arrow** points |

The mounting rule matters at rotation time: directional tires can't simply be swapped side to side on the same wheels. Our [rotation pattern tool](/tire-rotation-pattern) shows which patterns work for your setup.

## Two more labels you'll run into

- **Temporary spare.** Michelin lists **T** at the start of a size as the mark of a temporary spare. It's a get-you-to-a-shop tire with its own speed limit and pressure printed on it; see [spare tire types](/learn/damage/spare-tire-types).
- **EV tires.** Many electric cars come on tires tuned for weight, quietness and low rolling resistance, sometimes with higher load ratings. They're still one of the climate and driving types above; our guide to [EV tires](/learn/buying/ev-tires) explains what's different.

## A quick way to narrow it down

| If you drive... | Start with |
|---|---|
| A commuter car or crossover, mostly highway | Touring all-season |
| A sport sedan or a car that came on V, W or Y-rated tires | Performance all-season or summer |
| A pickup or SUV, all pavement | Highway (H/T) all-season |
| A pickup or SUV that leaves the pavement | All-terrain, often LT |
| A car that came on run-flats with no spare | Run-flats, or a plan for a flat |

That's a starting point, not the answer. The answer starts with your door placard.

## Whatever the type, match the placard

A tire type is a choice. The size, load index and speed rating are not. Replacement tires should match the size on your door placard and **meet or exceed** its load index and speed rating.
