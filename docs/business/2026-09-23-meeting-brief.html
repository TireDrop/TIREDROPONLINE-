What Tire Guru actually needs to do

Every tire on the site is drop-shipped from ATD — you're not selling shop stock online. That makes the Tire Guru question much simpler than it looks.

You need one thing: when someone buys online, that order should land in Tire Guru by itself. Then the sale is on your books, the install is on the schedule, and the shop floor sees the job without anyone re-keying it.

That's the whole ask. Not live inventory, not a two-way sync — just orders in. Worth knowing, because it's a much smaller request than it first sounds, and a much harder one to say no to.

Ask ATD — follow the order

These run in the order a sale happens: catalog, price, order, ship, returns. Asking them in sequence makes the conversation easy to follow and makes gaps obvious.

"If I build my own website, can I still get your tire data — or do I have to use yours?"

Step 1 of the sale. ATD has the list of every tire, its price, and how many are in the warehouse. Your site has to read that automatically — nobody is typing twenty thousand tires in by hand. But ATD sells its own website product, so they may only hand the data to people using it.

If the answer is no, this project stops here. That's why it's first.

Good"Yes — here's the spec and who to talk to."

FlagBeing steered to Radius or Net Driven as "the supported path." That's a sales answer, not an answer.

"Can you email me the full list of tire brands I can order from you right now?"

"Does your data tell me which tires fit which cars?"

Step 1, continued. Does your data know a 2019 F-150 takes a 275/65R18? That's called fitment, and it's what makes "shop by your vehicle" work — the most-used feature on any tire site. Some suppliers include it, some sell it separately, some don't have it. If ATD doesn't provide it, you buy it elsewhere, and that's a cost nobody has budgeted.

"When my website says a tire is in stock, is that right now — or is it last night's numbers?"

Step 2 — can I trust what I'm selling? If ATD only refreshes overnight, your site could sell a tire at 4pm that sold out at 9am. Customer books the install, the van shows up, no tire. Live numbers avoid that. Nightly is workable, but then the site says "usually in stock" instead of promising.

"Can my website send you an order by itself, or does somebody have to type it in?"

Step 3 of the sale. If it's not automatic, a staff member reads each web order and retypes it into ATD's portal. That's where wrong sizes and missed orders come from, and it's why shops stop trusting their own website. Automatic ordering is the difference between a store and an expensive brochure.

"When a customer buys on my site, where does the tire actually ship — to my shop, or to their house?"

Step 4 — and the one that shapes your whole operation.

To your shop: you control it. Tires arrive at Sunrise, the van loads up, you install on your schedule. This is what your mobile model is built around.

To the customer's house: they have to be home to take four tires, store them in the garage, and you're coordinating two deliveries instead of one. Some customers like it. Most would rather you just handled it.

Ask whether you can choose per order — shop for local installs, customer for anyone outside your service area. That's the setup you want.

Good"Either — you pick the destination when you place the order."

FlagShop-only when you wanted flexibility, or customer-only when your model is mobile install.

"Who pays the shipping, how long does it take, and what happens if a tire shows up wrong or damaged?"

Step 5 — the parts that bite later. Shipping cost decides whether you can price against SimpleTire and Tire Rack. Delivery time decides what your site can promise — "installed Thursday" and "installed sometime next week" are very different offers.

Returns matter more than people expect. If the wrong size lands at a customer's house, someone collects it, sends it back and reorders — and the customer is looking at you, not ATD. Settle whose problem that is now.

"Is there anything I have to sign or commit to in order to get this data?"

The catch. Data access often comes tied to a minimum monthly spend, an exclusivity clause, or a term you can't exit. None of ATD's terms are published anywhere, so the only way to know is to ask before you're invested.

Ask Tire Guru — getting orders in

Tire Guru isn't a supplier. It's the software running your register, and the only thing you need from it is for online orders to arrive by themselves. Be specific — a vague "do you have an API" gets a vague yes.

"When someone buys a tire on my website, can that order drop straight into Tire Guru?"

Why this matters: the order has to reach ATD so the tire ships, and it has to reach Tire Guru so the sale is recorded and the install is on the schedule. If Tire Guru can't take it automatically, somebody types every web order in by hand — twice a day, every day, forever. That's where wrong sizes and missed installs come from, and it's why shops stop trusting their own website.

"Solid Commerce and Tireweb both say they connect Tire Guru to outside websites. Do those work, and what do they cost?"

Other people have already done this. Both companies publicly advertise connecting Tire Guru to websites it didn't build. Naming them makes "that isn't possible" a much harder answer to give. If they confirm those work, you have a path even if Tire Guru won't build one for you.

"If I use a website you didn't build, will you still help me connect to it?"

Are you going to help or slow me down? Tire Guru sells its own websites, and its parent company owns a web design agency too. Two reasons to prefer you didn't do this. Not dishonest, just their business — but get the answer now, on the record, not six months in when you need support.

"What do I need from you to get started, and does it cost anything?"

What does it take to begin? Usually a key — a password that lets your site talk to theirs — and somewhere safe to test before going live. If there's a fee, find out today. No key means no build, whatever anyone promises.

Before you tell Net Driven anything

No public Net Driven contract terms or offboarding policy were found, and no reported exit disputes either — that's absence of information, not proof of a clean exit. Your signed agreement is the only authority. Pull it out before the call.

"Whose name is my website address registered under — mine or yours?"

Do I actually own extrememobiletires.com? Website companies sometimes register the domain in their own name. If Net Driven holds it, they control whether you can move — and a routine switch becomes a negotiation.

Check this yourself before the call, in your registrar account. If it's in your name, relax. If it isn't, fix that before you mention leaving.

"Can you send me a copy of everything on my site — the page addresses, the text, the photos, and any customer messages?"

Can I take my website with me? The critical piece is the page addresses. Google spent years learning that extrememobiletires.com/tires is your tire page. If the new site uses different addresses and the old ones break, you drop out of local search and the phone stops ringing. The old address list is what lets us point them at the new ones.

Ask for it as routine housekeeping, not as an exit signal.

"When does my contract end, when does it renew, and how much notice do I have to give?"

When can I leave without paying for a year I don't want? Most website contracts renew automatically unless you give notice in a specific window. Miss it and you're locked in another term. Know the dates before you schedule the switch, so the new site goes live as the old contract ends.

One structural note: Net Driven's tire catalog and pricing come through its distributor partnerships, ATD's included. That feed belongs to the partner program, not to you — so a site you own needs its own data source regardless. That's the real reason this project needs ATD to say yes.

Don't agree to

  • A developer registering accounts in their own name. Hosting, domain, database, payments — all in Extreme Tires' name, developer invited as a collaborator. Recovering these from a former contractor is the most common way a small business loses its own website.
  • Source code living in someone else's repository. Your account, your ownership, from the first day.
  • Switching the domain without a redirect map. Net Driven has years of local ranking attached to specific page addresses. Export them and point them at the new pages before the switch, or the phone stops ringing.
  • Cancelling Net Driven on switch day. Keep it live a month as the fallback, and export everything before it closes.
  • Tying the whole store to one supplier. Two ATD bankruptcies in six years, and a brand roster that changed a lot in one year. Build so a second supplier drops in without starting over — U.S. AutoForce is the strongest option and carries the brands ATD lost. TireHub, NTW (Palm Beach Gardens) and K&M Tire also serve Florida.
  • A timeline before ATD access is confirmed. Any date quoted today is a guess until someone has pulled a real price out of a real ATD test account.

The cheap way to de-risk this

If the meeting ends without a clear commitment, propose this instead of a full build: a short paid engagement that does nothing but connect to an ATD test account and pull one real tire and one real live price. It proves the riskiest assumption in the project for a fraction of the cost. If it works, commit with confidence. If it doesn't, you've lost almost nothing — and you found out before signing.
